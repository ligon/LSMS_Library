Measured after wiring =crop_production=, =plot_inputs=, =livestock=, =labor=
and =plot_labor= (PR #934).  All figures from the built tables, not the raw
files.  Re-runnable: =within2.py= in this directory.

* The estimable sample

Household-year aggregates: output = the four Section 9B disposition values
(sold + seed + given + lost); land = sum of =crop_production.Area_ha=; family
labour = =WeeksPerYear x HoursPerWeek= on =labor= rows that are own-farm AND
agricultural; hired labour = =plot_labor.Cost= where =source='hired'=; input
cost = =plot_inputs.Cost=.

| specification                                   | households | hh-years |
|-------------------------------------------------+------------+----------|
| CORE: output, land, family labour               |        504 |    1,008 |
| FULL: + hired labour + purchased inputs         |        232 |      464 |

Both are balanced two-wave panels, so the within estimator is exactly first
differences and Mundlak's group means are =(x_i1 + x_i2)/2=.

Within-household share of log variance (=SSW/SST=), on the panel sample:

| variable      | CORE  | FULL  |
|---------------+-------+-------|
| output        | 0.228 | 0.208 |
| area_ha       | 0.531 | 0.533 |
| family_hours  | 0.202 | 0.186 |
| hired_cost    |   --  | 0.280 |
| input_cost    |   --  | 0.269 |

There is identifying variation in every regressor.

* Three caveats, in order of how much they should worry you

** 1. Output is PARTIAL, and missing non-randomly

The four dispositions do NOT include what the household ate.  Section 9 never
asks it; it is Section 12B, already derived as
=GhanaLSS::food_acquired::12b-fortnight=, but joining it needs a crop <-> food
item mapping and crosses a recall window (annual vs annualised fortnight).

Measured earlier: *about 19% of cropped rows carry no Section 9 value at all*
-- crops grown and wholly consumed -- covering ~14% of harvested acres and
touching roughly half the farming households.  So the output measure is
systematically thinner for subsistence-oriented farms.  That is a *selected*
measure, not merely a noisy one, and it biases anything that correlates with
market orientation.

** 2. The FULL spec is a selected subsample

The drop from 504 to 232 households is not attrition, it is the log.  Of the
CORE household-years, *29.8% report no hired labour and 24.7% no purchased
input spend* -- true zeros, not gaps.  Requiring positive values to take logs
therefore conditions on being a commercially-input-using farm.

The honest fixes are to keep the zeros (levels, or a use-indicator plus an
intensity term) rather than to drop them.  Do not read the FULL spec as the
same population as the CORE one.

** 3. The land variable moves implausibly, and should be checked FIRST

=area_ha= has a within-household standard deviation of *1.12 in logs* -- a
typical household's reported cultivated area changes by a factor of about
three between the two waves.  Land holdings do not.

The likely mechanism is definitional rather than real: Q2 asks acres harvested
*per crop*, and the household total is a sum over the crops reported that year,
so a household reporting three crops in one round and five in the next shows a
large "area" change that is partly a change in what was enumerated.  A within
estimator is precisely what this contaminates, and land is the regressor whose
coefficient the exercise is most likely to be about.

*Check this before trusting any land elasticity.*  Cheap diagnostics: the
distribution of crops-reported-per-household across the two waves, and the
area change restricted to households reporting the SAME crop set.

* Verdict

Yes for the CORE specification: 504 households, two waves, identifying
variation in all three regressors, all from canonical tables.  Treat the FULL
specification as a different population, and settle caveat 3 before reading the
land coefficient.

* Comparing family and hired labour

They arrive in different units -- family labour as annual HOURS (=labor=),
hired labour as CEDIS (=plot_labor=), exchange labour as PERSON-DAYS -- so a
comparison needs a wage.  One is recoverable from =labor= itself: rows that are
agricultural and NOT own-farm are agricultural WAGE workers, and they carry
=Earnings= with an =EarningsUnit=.

** The wage: use the DAY rate

*CORRECTED 2026-09-16.*  A first pass put the Month / Week / Year-paid workers
on an hourly basis and DROPPED the 208 =Day=-paid observations, because Parts
B/C carry usual hours per week but no days per week.  That was backwards.
Agricultural labour is paid *per day* unless a document says otherwise
(@ligon), so the dropped observations were the representative ones and the
retained ones are largely salaried positions -- a different labour market.

The day rate needs no conversion at all:

| wave    |   n | median cedis/day | p25 | p75 |
|---------+-----+------------------+-----+-----|
| 1987-88 |  83 |              250 | 200 | 400 |
| 1988-89 | 125 |              300 | 200 | 400 |

*+20% nominal between the rounds*, which is what a high-inflation period should
show.  The first pass gave 40.65 -> 41.19, a flat +1.3%, and that anomaly was
flagged at the time without its cause being found: it was the wrong
subsample, not a data defect.

Median hours per day on agricultural work is *5.0* (n = 3,849), from the Parts
E/G rows -- the only ones that ask.  So:

| route                                    | 1987-88 | 1988-89 |
|------------------------------------------+---------+---------|
| A  day rate / hours per day (AG LABOUR)   |   50.00 |   60.00 |
| B  salaried workers, hourly (first pass)  |   40.65 |   41.11 |
| ratio B/A                                 |    0.81 |    0.69 |

The salaried route understates the agricultural wage by 19% and 31%.

** What it shows, in PERSON-DAYS

Person-days is the corpus's own unit for =plot_labor=, so the comparison is
reported there.  Family days = annual hours / 5; hired days = cost / day rate.

| wave    | family days | hired days |
|---------+-------------+------------|
| 1987-88 |       371.2 |       14.0 |
| 1988-89 |       284.2 |       16.0 |

Among the 3,230 household-years reporting both, the median family share of
person-days is *0.954* -- hired labour is about 5% of farm labour.  That share
is robust to the wage choice in direction though not in level: a higher wage
raises it, because family labour scales with the wage and hired cost does not.

** Family labour valued at the wage now EXCEEDS output

| wave    | family value | Section 9B output | ratio | (first pass) |
|---------+--------------+-------------------+-------+--------------|
| 1987-88 |  296,984,150 |       237,335,873 |  1.25 |         1.02 |
| 1988-89 |  288,163,080 |       206,631,067 |  1.39 |         0.96 |

Valuing family time at the market wage more than exhausts measured farm output.
That is the familiar smallholder result -- the shadow wage lies below the market
wage -- and the correction makes it stronger, not weaker.

It remains an UPPER bound on the ratio: the output measure is partial (Section 9
never asks own consumption; ~19% of cropped rows carry no Section 9 value at
all), so true output is higher and the true ratio lower.

** Residual caveats

1. *The day-wage sample is small*: 83 and 125 observations.  Only 716 of 23,585
   agricultural person-job rows are non-own-farm (3%).
2. *Hours per day comes from a different population* than the wage -- the Parts
   E/G rows, i.e. people whose 12-month job differed from their 7-day job.  It
   is used to convert between days and hours in both directions, so an error in
   it moves the hourly wage and the family-day count together.
3. *Family days of 371 per household-year* is several members working part-time,
   not one person working 371 days.  Do not read it as a person-equivalent.
4. The person-day share and any value share are not independent evidence: one
   wage converts both sides.
