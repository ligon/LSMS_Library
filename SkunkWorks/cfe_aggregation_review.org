* Assessment

The central intuition is correct: summing expenditures averages the
constituent Frisch elasticities, with expenditure shares as weights. Equal
elasticities preserve the loading on welfare exactly when their expenditure
coefficients are fixed, including zero coefficients; similar elasticities
introduce small curvature. This is a sound reason
to consider aggregation, especially where the current estimator discards
sparse goods.

The proposed objective does not yet measure its statistical cost. It drops
the noise variance, changes the population being evaluated, and leaves the
scale of welfare insufficiently specified. The coverage argument and the
Jaccard merge criterion also need correction. Constrained agglomeration is a
reasonable way to propose partitions, but its objective needs to be settled
before implementation.

The related preparation question is consequential. Executing the current
=prepare_data= and covariance-support helper on small constructed examples
shows avoidable household deletion and sensitivity to column order. An
aggregation comparison should benchmark against improved preparation as
well as the current routine, or it can credit aggregation for repairing a
filtering problem.

This review concerns the original aggregation notes introduced at LSMS
Library commit =769b43e0e=, as present at =71f1f7c55=, and the authoritative
Org source of CFEDemands at =e4fc38f=. References to original line numbers
below refer to =git show 769b43e0e:SkunkWorks/cfe_aggregation.org=.
The [[file:cfe_aggregation.org][current aggregation notes]] incorporate the revisions from this review.
It incorporates the maintained homoskedasticity assumption used to estimate
the fine-good elasticities. It does not assume that assumption is wrong.
No country-specific empirical comparison or aggregation implementation has
been performed.

* What summing preserves

Fix prices, demographics, and the item disturbances, and consider CFE
expenditures with \(a_j\geq0\) and a positive group total,
\[
  x_j(w)=a_j\exp(\beta_j w),\qquad
  h_G(w)=\log\sum_{j\in G}x_j(w),\qquad
  s_j(w)=x_j(w)/\sum_{k\in G}x_k(w).
\]
Direct differentiation gives
\[
  h_G'(w)=\sum_{j\in G}s_j(w)\beta_j,\qquad
  h_G''(w)=\sum_{j\in G}s_j(w)(\beta_j-h_G'(w))^2.
\]
These are identities, not approximations. They establish the user's
averaging argument and make its qualification precise: the weights, and
therefore the aggregate elasticity, generally vary with welfare, prices,
and demographics. A single fitted group coefficient is an approximation to
this varying loading, not generally the arithmetic mean of the fitted
constituent coefficients. Survey sampling weights are not these expenditure
shares.

If every constituent has the same \(\beta_G\), then
\(h_G(w)=\beta_G w+\log\sum_j a_j\) exactly. For approximately equal
elasticities, let \(\Delta_G=\max_j\beta_j-\min_j\beta_j\). On any interval
\(|w-w_0|\leq L\), the tangent approximation obeys
\[
  0\leq h_G(w)-h_G(w_0)-h_G'(w_0)(w-w_0)
  \leq \Delta_G^2(w-w_0)^2/8.
\]
The bound follows from the variance bound \(h_G''\leq\Delta_G^2/4\).
It supplies an economically interpretable tolerance: choose an allowable
error in log expenditure over a specified, commonly normalized welfare
range. It is not itself a bound on the error of estimated welfare.

Two limitations remain even with equal elasticities. First, when
\(a_j=\exp(\alpha_{jtm}+\gamma_j'd)\), the remaining term
\(\log\sum_j\exp(\alpha_{jtm}+\gamma_j'd)\) need not be linear in
demographics or additively separable between demographics and market-period
effects. Equal \(\gamma_j\) is sufficient to preserve that particular form;
more flexible controls are another possibility. Second, the calculation
holds the expenditure coefficients fixed as welfare varies. It includes
zero coefficients, but does not cover arbitrary reporting or participation
changes that make those coefficients depend on welfare.

* Zeros, household production, and aggregation

The expenditure data generally cannot distinguish true zeros from missing
responses. We adopt the survey convention that unreported item expenditures
within its expenditure module are zero, allowing that some conceal
unreported positive amounts. The zero mask consequently marks unavailable
log observations without classifying their causes. The
[[file:/home/ligon/Projects/CovariateRiskSharing/Text/risk-sharing.org::2349][risk-sharing application]] uses this convention for unreported food items.
An aggregation procedure need not first identify a distinction the data
cannot provide.

The [[file:/home/ligon/Research/VESDemand/Text/estimating_imue.org::451][household-production section of estimating_imue.org]] gives a stronger
economic argument than the positive-demand calculation alone. Inputs to
a common composite \(r\) have expenditures
\(x_{rj}=\eta_{rj}(p)b_r(p,z)\exp(\beta_r w)\), where the nonnegative
input share \(\eta_{rj}\) can be zero. Summing any fixed subset with
positive total expenditure preserves \(\beta_r\) exactly. Changes in
input choice across prices can produce complementary zero patterns while
the inputs retain a common Frisch elasticity. The
[[file:/home/ligon/Projects/CovariateRiskSharing/Text/risk-sharing.org::298][risk-sharing paper]] uses the same production interpretation. This provides
a structural case for grouping substitutes; knowing that two labels refer
to similar foods is still insufficient to establish this structure.

The [[file:/home/ligon/Research/VESDemand/Text/estimating_imue.org::151][estimation discussion]] allows zeros or unavailable expenditure observations
under a missing-at-random condition /conditional/ on shadow prices,
observed characteristics, and latent welfare. Zero patterns may therefore
vary with welfare; dependence on welfare alone does not contradict that
condition. This does not establish that arbitrary underreporting is
harmless, or that summing reported amounts preserves the required error
moments. Such deviations warrant sensitivity analysis. They do not justify
requiring the implementation to recover separate zero and nonresponse
masks. My earlier requirement to distinguish them was too strong.

* Homoskedasticity and the information calculation

Start from the maintained fine-good assumption
\(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_c)=\sigma^2 I\), conditional on the
factor and controls. For small log disturbances, a Taylor expansion of the
log of summed expenditure gives
\[
  e_{Gc}\simeq\sum_{j\in G}s^0_{jc}\varepsilon_{jc},\qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(e_{Gc})\simeq\sigma^2\sum_{j\in G}(s^0_{jc})^2.
\]
Here \(s^0\) denotes shares at the systematic expenditures about which the
expansion is taken. Using noisy realized shares as fixed weights would
overstate the exactness of this variance formula.

Thus aggregation can generate heteroskedasticity even if the initial
assumption holds exactly. Equal shares in a group of size \(K\) reduce its
error variance to approximately \(\sigma^2/K\); a singleton retains
\(\sigma^2\). Shares that vary across households also produce household
variation in aggregate error variance. Assuming common noise again after
aggregation is an additional restriction, not a consequence of the
fine-good model. Equal group sizes and equal within-group shares provide a
special case where the first-order variances do remain common.

This invalidates the precision argument in original line 40, which considers
the change in loadings without the accompanying change in noise. In the
idealized calculation with known nuisance terms, two independent goods
with \(\beta_1=\beta_2=1\), equal expenditure shares, and noise variance
\(0.01\) each provide information \(2/0.01=200\). Their sum provides
\(1/0.005=200\) to first order. The proposed loss nevertheless rises from
\(1/2\) to \(1\).

Equal elasticities do not guarantee negligible statistical cost either.
Under the same assumptions, shares \((0.99,0.01)\) give aggregate error
variance \(0.9802\sigma^2\), against \(0.5\sigma^2\) for the efficient
fine-good estimate. The variance nearly doubles despite exact equality of
the elasticities. The sum gives most weight to the larger expenditure;
the efficient fine-good estimator can use both noisy log observations.

More generally, conditional on known nuisance terms, local information in
a summed group satisfies
\[
  I_G\simeq\frac{(s'\beta)^2}{s'\Sigma s}
  \leq\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\beta=I_{\rm fine},
\]
for positive definite \(\Sigma\). Equality requires
\(s\propto\Sigma^{-1}\beta\). Under homoskedasticity and equal positive
elasticities this reduces to equal shares. This is a local oracle benchmark,
not an argument against aggregation: a finite-sample estimator with estimated
loadings, sparse observations, and hard inclusion thresholds can benefit
from grouping. It specifies where the benefit must come from.

The default beta estimator explicitly assumes spherical residual noise in
[[file:../../CFEDemands/Empirics/regression.org::450][regression.org]]. Its existing =heteroskedastic= option is a relevant
sensitivity comparison, not a substitute for evaluating aggregation.
[[https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08316][Zhang, Cai, and Wu's HeteroPCA]] addresses heterogeneous diagonal noise;
it does not by itself correct nonlinear group loadings, endogenous
participation, or arbitrary correlated item errors.

* The loss must compare the same estimand and population

The loss in original line 43 has four distinct problems.

1. It omits \(\sigma_A^2\), which cannot be canceled across partitions.
   Homoskedasticity within each partition would not make its value the same
   between partitions. With diagonal heterogeneous noise the conditional
   GLS information is \(\sum_g\beta_g^2/\sigma_g^2\); with general noise
   covariance it is \(\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\beta\).
2. It is sensitive to factor normalization:
   \(\beta_A\mapsto c\beta_A\), \(w_A\mapsto w_A/c\) multiplies the
   proposed loss by \(c^{-2}\) without changing fitted expenditures.
   The suggested variance normalization needs a fixed reference population
   and an explicit convention. Normalizing each noisy fitted factor on its
   own retained sample does not fix a common latent target. Keeping common
   anchor goods unmerged is one possible way to impose a common loading
   scale.
3. Averaging over \(N_A\) rewards dropping difficult households. Fix the
   target household-period population and its weights before comparing
   partitions. Report coverage by wave, market, and relevant demographic
   strata. Use a fixed common evaluation sample for direct accuracy
   comparisons and disclose performance and coverage outside it.
4. It measures conditional variance, not bias from approximate aggregation,
   uncertainty in estimated loadings and controls, or uncertainty induced by
   choosing the partition. A small reported standard error need not mean a
   good welfare estimate.

The conceptual target is mean squared error for a fixed normalization of
\(w=-\log\lambda\), with explicitly chosen population weights. A scalar
criterion also needs a cost for failing to return a household's estimate.
Until that cost is specified, a frontier of coverage and validated error is
more informative than claiming one statistically optimal partition.

There is also an estimator mismatch. Original line 31 is the diagonal GLS
score with nuisance terms known. =Regression.get_w= calls =estimate_w=,
which fits a joint, unweighted least-squares system including market-period
intercepts. The existing =w_cov= accounts for those intercepts under its
homoskedastic, known-beta model. Its denominator becomes singular when all
observed loadings are equal within a market. Preserving similarity /within/
groups should not remove the variation /between/ groups needed by this
estimator. Within-market contrasts and welfare levels across markets also
have different identification requirements.

* Coverage and support need a corrected definition

The user specifies that every element of \(X_0\) is nonnegative. Take
\(X_0\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{J_0\times N_0}\) as the mathematical input,
with zeros interpreted as above.
For a group \(G\), nonnegativity gives the exact identity
\[
  \mathbf{1}\{\sum_{j\in G}X_{0,jc}>0\}
  =\mathbf{1}\{\exists j\in G:X_{0,jc}>0\}.
\]
Its support is the union of its constituents' supports, with no possibility
of cancellation. Equivalently, with \(O_0=\mathbf{1}\{X_0>0\}\),
\(O_A=\mathbf{1}\{AO_0>0\}\). The notes' \(M_A\) is a valid zero mask:
\(O_A=1-M_A\), and \(M_{A,Gc}=\prod_{j\in G}M_{0,jc}\).

With goods in rows,
\(\sum_g O_{gc}\) counts usable log observations for household-period
\(c\), and \(OO'\), of dimension \(J\times J\), counts joint observations
of pairs of goods. In the notes' notation that matrix is
\((1-M_A)(1-M_A)'\), not \(M_A'M_A\), a household-by-household count of
joint zeros. To retain households with at least \(\underline J\) positive
groups, the zero-mask criterion is
\(\sum_g M_{A,gc}\leq J-\underline J\). The deletion rule in the notes
uses the opposite direction and does not make this conversion.

Require each original good to belong to exactly one nonempty group:
\(\sum_g A_{gj}=1\) and \(\sum_j A_{gj}\geq1\). If exclusion of goods is
allowed, represent and penalize it separately. Column deletion is right
multiplication by a selection matrix once selected; the rule choosing it
from the data is not a fixed linear operator as written in original line 10.

Under this nonnegative-input premise, aggregation cannot increase
a household's number of positive entries. A merge replaces two positives
with one, one positive with one, or two zeros with zero. What can improve
is the number of households buying /a group/, its overlap with other groups,
and survival after CFE drops unsupported goods. A threshold expressed as a
fraction of the number of goods can also change survival. These are the
relevant mechanisms; original line 39 should not describe an expansion in
the household's active-set cardinality.

The current =prepare_data= recursively applies household item-fraction
filters, matches demographics, and calls =drop_columns_wo_covariance=.
The latter needs adequate paired observations for /every/ covariance entry
of the retained goods. Counting how many pairs pass a threshold is
insufficient. The stopping rule further conflates a count of acceptable
pairs with the minimum number of observations /per pair/. Assess candidate
partitions through the actual preparation routine and record its exclusions.
Support counts alone do not establish factor identification or eliminate
selection bias.

LSMS =food_expenditures= drops zero and missing values and defaults to
purchases. Within a defined survey item universe, reconstruct the
nonnegative expenditure table using the convention above; its sparse
output need not supply a separate nonresponse mask. Acquisition basis,
recall comparability, market definition, source aggregation, and known
questionnaire coverage must be fixed before selecting food groups.
Conditional observation assumptions concern estimation from this table;
making it denser does not establish them.

* Merge proposals should respect approximation error and uncertainty

The studentized difference in original line 49 measures evidence against
equality. It does not measure the cost of imposing approximate equality.
A large standard error can make very different point estimates look close.
Conversely, an economically negligible difference will be rejected with
enough data. Use an equivalence tolerance or a loss based on the curvature
bound, with uncertainty about the elasticities carried into that assessment.

Differences from a joint fit require
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j-\hat\beta_k)
  =V_{jj}+V_{kk}-2V_{jk}.
\]
Normalization must be consistent across bootstrap draws and fits. A pilot
on common goods cannot supply identified elasticities for excluded sparse
goods, and a pilot on broad groups cannot identify its constituent loadings.
Semantic assignments for such goods remain assumptions to disclose and
validate, not passed beta tests.

The repo's existing [[file:../.claude/skills/add-feature/food-acquired/SKILL.md::419][50-percent beta-spread rule]] is prior practice, but it
does not prove approximate equivalence or preservation of welfare. Its
threshold shrinks with sample size rather than with an acceptable economic
error, and it ignores the covariance of the estimates. This is a substantive
objection to documented guidance, not an assumption that the guidance is
stale.

The claim at [[file:../.claude/skills/add-feature/food-acquired/SKILL.md::507][lines 507-509]] concerns predicting /held-out/ nonfood
expenditures: it says better prediction means a better MUE estimate. This
needs qualification because it depends on how the outcome and its errors
relate to welfare and tastes. It is not a claim that including nonfood
expenditures in factor estimation improves precision, and is not attributed
here to the author of the aggregation notes.

The user's experience in Uganda is that adding nonfood expenditures to
estimation produced highly implausible results. Different survey
instruments, with error variances that violate homoskedasticity, are a
proposed explanation. That result has not been reproduced in this review,
but is sufficient reason to keep the initial aggregation exercise within
foods. More goods improve the oracle information calculation only under
its maintained error model; they need not improve a fit that incorrectly
imposes common error variances. Heterogeneous variances also call for care
when weighting any held-out prediction diagnostic, even though that
exercise does not put nonfood errors into the estimated factor.

For a positive coefficient on the Jaccard /distance/, minimizing original line 49
favors overlapping supports. Disjoint supports have distance one and
identical supports distance zero. This is opposite to original line 56. Reversing
the sign alone does not establish a welfare benefit; measure the change in
usable support after preparation instead. Complementarity can reflect
substitution, but it can also reflect geography, wave-specific questionnaires,
or sharply different household types.

The sign rule at original line 52 also mislabels the economic distinction.
Positive Frisch elasticities do not define luxuries: necessities can have
positive loadings too. Unexpected negative estimates deserve diagnosis;
their sampling noise should not be turned into an unquestioned hard rule.

Food semantics are useful constraints on the admissible partitions.
Taxonomies and curator restrictions offer an interpretable starting point;
embeddings can propose candidates. A corpus of short food labels is not by
itself a reliable training sample for an embedding model. Semantic proximity
does not guarantee equal elasticities or comparable recall and preparation
status. Pairwise admissibility also needs a group-level rule to prevent a
chain of individually acceptable merges from producing an incoherent group.

A greedy tree explores only one set of partitions. Refit and evaluate its
candidate cuts; improvement need not be monotone, so stopping at the first
worsening need not find even the best cut on that tree. The assertion of
NP-hardness in original line 47 needs a specified optimization problem and
an argument. The number of partitions alone establishes a large search
space, not a complexity theorem.

* The related preparation problem

There are two questions: which observations support estimating common
parameters, and for which households those fitted parameters support an
estimate of welfare. The current =prepare_data= uses one surviving sample
for both. Its thresholds are useful numerical safeguards, but do not define
an optimal welfare-estimation sample.

** A reproduced ordering problem

At [[file:../../CFEDemands/Empirics/regression.org::1029][regression.org:1029]], the expression
=Y.loc[:,Y.count()>min_obs]= has no assignment, so the intended early
marginal-support filter does nothing. The household item-fraction filter
runs before the later covariance filter discards sparse goods. Recursion
then works only with the reduced sample; it cannot restore households that
would qualify against the final, smaller number of goods.
The downstream helper admits counts equal to =min_obs=. Simply assigning
the existing expression, which uses a strict inequality, would introduce a
different boundary; the two filters should implement one stated rule.

A deterministic example with the default =min_obs=30= and
=min_prop_items=0.1= has 60 households and 20 goods. Four goods have enough
observations; 16 are observed only once. Forty households report the four
common goods, and twenty report two of them. The first household filter
requires more than two goods and deletes those twenty households.

The current function returns 40 households and four goods. Removing the
16 goods that already fail marginal support, then calling /the same
function/, returns 60 households and the same four goods. This is an
algorithmic loss of coverage, not evidence that economic aggregation was
necessary. It does not prove that the larger sample yields unbiased or more
precise estimates under every selection process; it establishes that those
households can satisfy the final preparation criteria.

** A reproduced support-selection problem

For a fixed set of households, make a graph with an edge between goods
whose paired observations meet =min_obs=. Requiring every retained
covariance to be estimable means choosing a clique in this graph.
=drop_columns_wo_covariance= repeatedly deletes the good with the fewest
available covariance entries. This is a heuristic, and ties depend on
column order.

In a constructed seven-good example with 30 observations per supported
pair, the helper keeps goods ={4,6}= even though ={0,1,6}= meets the
identical support requirement. Reversing the input columns returns
={6,1,0}=. The example and its checks are reproduced below. The larger
clique is not automatically the best welfare estimator either; the point
is that the existing rule optimizes neither retained goods nor welfare
coverage or precision.

For fixed households, maximizing the number of retained goods is a
maximum-clique problem. Jointly choosing goods and households changes the
support graph and complicates that problem. A deterministic tie-break
improves reproducibility, but does not establish optimality. Any replacement
search needs an explicit retention/information objective.

** Separate fitting eligibility from scoring eligibility

With beta and the relevant nuisance terms estimated from an adequately
supported training sample, a household can have a usable factor score even
if it is unsuitable for training the covariance matrix. Under the maintained
homoskedastic model, its conditional information is
\(\sum_{j\in O_c}\beta_j^2/\sigma^2\), with the additional uncertainty
from fitted nuisance terms accounted for. A fraction-of-all-goods cutoff
is only a rough proxy for this quantity: it counts weak and strong loadings
equally and changes when unrelated sparse labels are added.

Even a single-good household can be scored conditional on an estimable
nonzero loading and its market/nuisance effects. It contributes no
independent information for separating the common market intercept from
its own welfare, and cannot estimate its own residual variance. The
current =w_cov(min_goods=1)= and
=w_var(cov='homoskedastic', min_goods=1)= already recognize the latter
case using pooled variance; this is a conditional capability, not proof
that a market consisting entirely of such households is identified.

Thus a promising improvement is to preserve the original observations,
select a reliable parameter-fitting sample, then score a broader eligible
population with frozen loadings, controls, and market effects. This needs
an explicit scoring path: merely lowering =min_prop_items= or passing the
broader sample back through =Regression= would refit the parameters and
change the comparison. Missing demographics or unestimated market effects
remain reasons a score may be unavailable.

The same distinction permits a later extension for sparse goods: their
loadings might be estimated against a factor identified by common goods,
with uncertainty propagated. That is a different estimator from the
current complete-covariance route and should be evaluated separately from
summing goods.

The direct implementation priorities are therefore to expose and log
selection decisions, repair the ineffective early filter, reconsider
irreversible deletion against a changing denominator, and distinguish
training from scoring. Compare these preparation variants at fixed
aggregation before optimizing the aggregation itself. Keep =alltm= and
the meaning of =min_obs= explicit: the former requires presence across
market-period cells, while the latter currently governs paired counts
pooled over retained household-period observations. Neither is by itself
a precision guarantee.

* An implementation direction justified by this review

1. Fix the country, expenditure basis, household-period universe, market
   definition, factor normalization, and questionnaire coverage. Use the
   stated zero convention and conditional observation assumptions.
   Retain the fine data and an explicit, exhaustive membership map.
2. Benchmark the current and revised preparation rules at a fixed
   partition, distinguishing parameter-fitting coverage from scoring
   coverage. Preserve the original data for reconsideration of exclusions.
3. Generate economically coherent candidate merges, constrained by an
   explicit tolerance for beta dispersion and documented treatment of
   unidentified sparse items. Use the existing hand-curated partition and
   no aggregation as comparison cases.
4. Aggregate expenditures before logging, rerun the actual preparation and
   estimation stages, and report coverage, paired support, between-group
   loading variation, residual variances, and conditional uncertainty.
   Compare all candidates on a common scale and evaluation population.
5. Validate beyond their fitted standard errors. Use simulation with known
   welfare to measure bias, variance, MSE, and interval coverage. Include
   equal and unequal loadings, balanced and concentrated shares, realistic
   sparsity, and demographic effects. Include substitution among inputs to
   a common composite, and sensitivity to unreported positive amounts and
   departures from the conditional observation assumptions. On real data, select
   partitions on training households and evaluate on held-out households;
   keep a household's panel observations together. For prediction of
   reserved goods, exclude those goods from that household's factor score.
   Otherwise prediction partly scores a variable against itself. Treat
   nonfood prediction as a qualified diagnostic, accounting for its different
   measurement process; it does not establish welfare accuracy. Keep nonfood
   expenditures out of the initial factor-estimation exercise.
6. Report the coverage/error frontier and partition stability. Resampling
   that is intended to include selection uncertainty must repeat selection
   and fitting, with household or survey-cluster dependence respected.
   Conditional inference for an already fixed map is a narrower target.

This supports starting implementation with an evaluator of supplied
partitions, then adding a constrained search. It does not require settling
the best search heuristic before measuring the objective it should optimize.

#+begin_src python :tangle no :eval never-export
import numpy as np

b = np.ones(2)
sigma2 = 0.01
s = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
fine_information = (b @ b) / sigma2
group_information = (s @ b)**2 / (sigma2 * (s @ s))
assert np.isclose(fine_information, 200)
assert np.isclose(group_information, fine_information)

s = np.array([0.99, 0.01])
variance_ratio = (s @ s) / 0.5
assert np.isclose(variance_ratio, 1.9604)

# For beta=(0.5,1.5), log(exp(0.5w)+exp(1.5w))
# equals log(2)+w+log(cosh(w/2)).
assert np.isclose(np.log(np.cosh(1)), 0.4337808304830271)

O = np.array([[1, 0, 1, 1], [0, 1, 1, 1]])
assert (O @ O.T).tolist() == [[3, 2], [2, 3]]
assert np.all((O.sum(axis=0) > 0).astype(int) <= O.sum(axis=0))

# Equal loadings leave the joint intercept/score design singular.
assert np.isclose(len(b) - b.sum()**2/(b @ b), 0)

    [np.arange(N), np.zeros(N, int), np.zeros(N, int)],
    names=['i', 't', 'm'])
X = pd.DataFrame(np.nan, index=cells,
                 columns=pd.Index(['g%02d' % j for j in range(J)], name='j'))
X.iloc[:, :4] = np.arange(1, N+1)[:, None] + np.arange(4)[None, :]
X.iloc[40:, 2:4] = np.nan
for j in range(4, J):
    X.iloc[j-4, j] = j+1
D = pd.DataFrame({'size': 2 + np.arange(N) % 5}, index=cells)

prepare = scope['prepare_data']
y0, _ = prepare(np.log(X).stack().dropna(), D, alltm=False)
y1, _ = prepare(np.log(X.loc[:, X.count() >= 30]).stack().dropna(),
                D, alltm=False)
assert y0.index.droplevel('j').nunique() == 40
assert y1.index.droplevel('j').nunique() == 60
assert set(y0.index.get_level_values('j')) == set(y1.index.get_level_values('j'))

edges = [(0, 1), (0, 6), (1, 3), (1, 5), (1, 6),
         (2, 5), (2, 6), (3, 6), (4, 5), (4, 6)]
rows = []
for j, k in edges:
    for z in range(30):
        row = np.full(7, np.nan)
        row[[j, k]] = [1+z, 2+z]
        rows.append(row)
frame = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=list(range(7)))
drop = scope['drop_columns_wo_covariance']
assert list(drop(frame, min_obs=30).columns) == [4, 6]
assert list(drop(frame[frame.columns[::-1]], min_obs=30).columns) == [6, 1, 0]
assert frame[[0, 1, 6]].cov(min_periods=30).notna().all().all()
